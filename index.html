The short version

What a Ryoko Pro actually is

A router small enough for a coat pocket, with a mobile data connection of its own and a WiFi network it hands out to whatever you are carrying.

Think of it as the difference between borrowing and owning. A phone abroad borrows a foreign carrier's network and pays for the privilege. A laptop in a hotel borrows the hotel's network and accepts whatever the hotel's bandwidth and login page allow. Ryoko Pro does neither: it holds a SIM, finds the strongest local tower on its own, and broadcasts a network that only your devices join.

What follows from that is the whole product. Nothing to configure when you cross a border, because the device re-selects the tower itself. No per-device data plans, because one connection serves up to ten. No carrier bill arriving six weeks later, because your handset never touches a foreign cellular network at all.

The comparison

A Ryoko Pro against your carrier's roaming rates

This is the sum worth doing before you order, and it is a short one.

The Federal Communications Commission is blunt about what roaming costs. Its traveller guidance warns that rates may be much higher because of additional roaming fees on foreign mobile networks, and that for most United States customers a domestic plan does not cover usage abroad at all. It adds a detail that catches people out: phones and apps download data by themselves while the handset is simply switched on, so the meter runs without anybody opening a browser.

Then there is the timing. The same guidance notes that roaming charges may not appear on your bill for an additional billing cycle after your return, which is why the unpleasant surprise usually lands a month after the holiday.

So take your own carrier's published daily rate for the countries you visit, multiply by the days you will genuinely be away over the next year, and set the result against $89 once plus $0.66 a day of data. The FCC's own suggestion to travellers is to use Wi-Fi spots, because WiFi does not draw on a mobile allowance. A pocket router is that advice with the hotspot in your own bag.

One payment, and your handset never joins a foreign cellular network.

Security, precisely

What a Ryoko Pro does and does not protect

Carrying your own network removes one category of risk. It is worth being exact about which one, because this is the subject marketing pages routinely overstate.

What a Ryoko Pro genuinely fixes

The habit it breaks is joining networks about which you know precisely nothing. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency states the difficulty without decoration: although these Wi-Fi hotspots can be convenient, they are not always secure, potentially exposing you to online risks. Guidance from the National Security Agency aimed at people working in public arrives at the same place, cautioning that they could be putting their personal and organizational data, credentials, and devices at higher risk. Set against an unknown network, one carrying only hardware you own is a genuine step up.

What a Ryoko Pro does not do

It does not encrypt anything. On this the Federal Trade Commission is calmer than the folklore suggests: because of the widespread use of encryption, connecting through a public Wi-Fi network is usually safe. Whatever shields your traffic sits between browser and website — the FTC's quick test is a lock symbol or https in the address bar — and that shield is no stronger on your own router than on a hotel's. Swapping networks is simply not the same operation as encrypting what crosses them. It offers nothing whatsoever against a site run by a fraudster, which the FTC identifies as exactly the hazard encryption leaves open.

If you want the extra layer

Different tool, different job: that belongs to a VPN. FTC guidance holds that VPN apps can help shield personal information sent over a public, open Wi-Fi network, with four things to do first — research the app, review its permission requests, confirm it encrypts your information, and find out whether it passes that information to third parties. Buying a router is not buying a VPN. Anyone implying otherwise has something to sell.

Alternatives

A Ryoko Pro against a local SIM or a roaming add-on

Three ways to solve the same problem, each better than the others in a specific situation.

A local SIM is usually the cheapest data in any one country, and it is the right answer for a long stay in a single place. Against it: you buy it on arrival, often with a passport and a queue; it covers one device; and you repeat the whole exercise at the next border.

A carrier roaming add-on is the least effort of the three, because it is a button in an app. Against it: it is the most expensive per day, it covers one device, and the FCC's warning about charges surfacing a billing cycle late applies squarely to it.

A pocket router like this one is a single purchase that covers up to ten devices across 133 countries with nothing to buy on arrival. Against it: it is hardware you have to remember, charge and carry, there is a cost up front before any data, and in a country where you would have bought a cheap local SIM anyway it will not be the cheapest option.

That comparison is between three approaches rather than between named products, and nothing here is a claim about any particular competing device or network operator.

Buy once; ten devices, 133 countries, and nothing to arrange on landing.

Plain English

Ryoko Pro terms explained

Seven expressions that turn up on the listing, the checkout or the policy pages.

Mobile hotspot
A compact router holding a cellular connection of its own, which it republishes as WiFi for several devices to share. Ryoko Pro sits squarely in this class of hardware.
Roaming
Borrowing a foreign operator's network while still billed by your own carrier, almost always at a premium. Federal guidance notes that a domestic American plan frequently does not extend overseas at all.
Reference price
The "before" number against which a discount is measured. Four quantities, four of them here — $296.67, $460.00, $590.00 and $733.33 — each reduced by the same 70%.
Top-up
Buying more data once the 500MB supplied has gone. This is the recurring side of ownership, and it sits apart from whatever the hardware cost you.
Mb/s
Megabits per second, how mobile speed is conventionally quoted. Since a byte is eight bits, a 150 Mb/s ceiling corresponds to somewhere near 18 megabytes arriving each second.
Phishing domain
An address dressed to pass for a legitimate one, in the hope of harvesting a password or a card number. "playpal.com" impersonating "paypal.com" is the example the listing itself reaches for.
Original condition
What the support site's 60-day policy demands of a returned parcel, which in that wording means still sealed. It is also why the 30-day guarantee at the checkout is the more practically useful of the pair.
